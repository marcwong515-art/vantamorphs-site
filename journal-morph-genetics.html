Recessive vs Co-Dominant: Morph Genetics Made Simple

VANTAMORPHS 7 min read Sabah, Malaysia

Browse any breeder's collection and you will hit names like GHI Dhet VPI Axanthic Pied and wonder whether you need a genetics degree. You don't. Almost every ball python name follows a handful of simple rules, and once you know them the labels stop being jargon and start being information.

Genes come in pairs

A snake inherits one copy of each gene from each parent. So for any given trait it holds two copies. Whether that trait shows up in the animal's appearance depends on how those copies interact, and that is what breeders mean by "recessive", "co-dominant" and "dominant".

Everything below follows from that one fact.

Recessive: you need two copies

A recessive trait only appears when the animal inherits two copies, one from each parent. With just one copy, the snake looks completely normal but still carries the gene and can pass it on.

That carrier state is what "het" means, short for heterozygous. A het animal is visually unremarkable and genetically valuable.

Common recessive traits include Clown, Piebald, VPI Axanthic, Desert Ghost (you will see it written DG) and Lavender.

Reading our own labels

Look at three animals from our collection and the naming convention decodes itself:

  • Clown Het VPI Axanthic: visually a Clown (two copies of Clown), and carrying one hidden copy of VPI Axanthic.
  • Fire Lace YB Het Clown: you can see the Fire, Lace and Yellow Belly. The Clown is carried, not shown.
  • GHI Dhet VPI Axanthic Pied: Dhet is double het: one hidden copy each of two different recessives, VPI Axanthic and Piebald.

The rule of thumb: anything listed before "Het" is what you are looking at. Anything after it is what the animal is carrying.

What the pairings produce

Why "66% Het" exists

From a Het × Het clutch, the non-visual babies are each either het or normal, and you cannot tell by looking. Of those non-visual animals, two out of three are hets, so they are honestly sold as "66% possible het". It is a probability, not a guarantee. A "100% het" is different: its parentage guarantees the gene.

Co-dominant: one copy already shows

Here a single copy visibly changes the animal, and two copies produce a distinct, usually more extreme "super" form.

Because a single copy is visible, so there is no such thing as a "het Pastel". If the animal has the gene, you can see it. This is why co-dominant traits are the building blocks of complex combos: every gene you add announces itself.

A note on the word "co-dominant"

Strictly speaking, most of these traits are incomplete dominant, not co-dominant, in true co-dominance both versions appear side by side, whereas here they blend into an intermediate form. The hobby settled on "co-dom" decades ago and it stuck. Worth knowing if you read the scientific literature; nobody will correct you at a reptile expo.

Co-dominant pairings

Co-dom × Normal50% morph · 50% normal

Super × Normal100% single-copy morph

That last row is why supers are prized in a breeding project: a Super Pastel paired to anything produces a clutch where every baby is at least Pastel.

Dominant: no super form

A dominant trait looks the same whether the animal carries one copy or two. There is no visually distinct super. Spider and Pinstripe are the familiar examples.

One responsibility worth stating plainly: the Spider gene is linked to a neurological "wobble" that varies in severity between animals. It is well documented and something any honest breeder should raise with a buyer rather than leave them to discover.

Why the names get so long

Combine several genes in one animal and you list them all. Banana SN YB Clown is four traits stacked: Banana, Spotnose and Yellow Belly each contributing visibly, plus a visual Clown underneath.

Each added gene multiplies the possibilities in a clutch, which is exactly why designer projects take years. You are not chasing one trait. You are lining up several at once, and the odds compound.

A morph name is not marketing. It is a record of every decision made before that animal hatched.

Working the odds out yourself

You do not need software for this. A four-box grid. A Punnett square, covers almost every pairing you will do.

Write one parent's two gene copies across the top and the other parent's down the side, then fill in each box with the pair that results. Take Het Clown × Het Clown. Each parent carries one Clown copy (c) and one normal copy (N):

Nc
NNN, normalNc, het Clown
cNc, het Clowncc, visual Clown

Four boxes: one normal, two hets, one visual Clown. That is the 25 / 50 / 25 split quoted earlier, and you just derived it rather than memorising it.

The same grid works for co-dominant traits. The only difference is that Nc is now visible as the morph and cc is the super form. One tool, both cases.

When several genes are in play

Each gene sorts independently, so you multiply the odds. If a pairing gives a 1-in-4 chance of a visual Clown and a 1-in-2 chance of Pastel, the chance of a single baby being both is 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/8.

Stack four traits and the target combination can sit at 1-in-64 or worse. This is the real reason high-end animals cost what they do. Not scarcity for its own sake, but the number of clutches it takes to land one, plus the years of growing the parents to breeding size.

Odds are per egg, not per clutch

A 25% chance does not mean one in every four eggs. Each egg is an independent roll. A clutch of six can easily contain zero of what you were aiming for, or three. Breeders plan across seasons for exactly this reason.

Five things worth clearing up

  1. "Het" is not a morph. A het Clown is a normal-looking snake. If someone shows you a het and claims you can see it, be sceptical.
  2. A super is not simply "better". It is two copies of the same gene, which may look dramatically different. A Super Fire is a white snake with black eyes, not an intense Fire.
  3. Morphs are not hybrids. Every animal here is Python regius. These are naturally occurring mutations selectively bred, not crosses between species.
  4. Colour changes with age. Many morphs darken or "brown out" as they mature. A hatchling photo is not a promise about the adult.
  5. Two animals with identical names can look different. Line quality varies enormously between breeders. The name tells you the genetics, not the standard of the animal.

What this means when you buy

  • Visual traits are verifiable: you can see them in the animal in front of you.
  • Het claims rest entirely on the breeder's records. Ask about the pairing. A breeder who tracks lineage will answer immediately.
  • Understand "possible het" before paying for it. 66% and 50% possible hets are gambles with known odds. Fine if priced accordingly.
  • If you only want a beautiful pet: hets are irrelevant to you. Buy the animal you love and ignore the genetic suffix entirely.

That last point matters more than the rest. Genetics is what makes this hobby endlessly interesting for breeders, but a Pastel with no hidden genes is exactly as good a companion as one carrying four.
